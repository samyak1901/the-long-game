# How I Think About Compounding

Compounding is almost always explained with money, a dollar growing at some rate, doubling and doubling again until the numbers look absurd. That example is fine, but it badly undersells the idea. Compounding is not a finance trick. It's the basic shape of how advantages accumulate, and it shows up almost everywhere once you start looking.

## It's bigger than money

Money compounds, yes. But so do other things, often more powerfully:

- **Knowledge** compounds. What you learn this year makes next year's learning faster, because you have more hooks to hang new ideas on.
- **Taste and judgement** compound. Every decision you reflect on sharpens the next one.
- **Relationships and reputation** compound. Trust earned slowly becomes the thing that opens doors you didn't know existed.
- **Career capital** compounds. Skills, credibility, and a track record stack on top of each other.

The common thread is that the output of one period becomes the input to the next. That's the whole mechanism. Anything with that property, where today's gains raise tomorrow's starting line, compounds.

## Why we underestimate it

Human intuition is linear. We're good at "add a bit each year" and bad at "multiply a bit each year." So we consistently underestimate exponential processes, especially early on, when they look almost flat.

The trap is that compounding is boring for a long time and then suddenly isn't. Most of the dramatic growth lives in the back half of the curve. If a dollar grows at 10% for thirty years, the gains in the final few years dwarf everything that came before, not because the rate changed, but because the base got so large. *(Illustrative, not a forecast.)*

> The hard part isn't understanding the math. It's staying in your seat long enough for the back half of the curve to arrive.

That's why patience isn't a personality trait in investing; it's a structural requirement. You cannot harvest the back half of the curve if you keep cutting the process short.

## Small edges, repeated

I don't believe in finding one brilliant idea that changes everything. I believe in small, durable edges applied consistently over a long time.

A business that earns slightly higher returns on capital and reinvests them. A decision process that's a little more honest than average. A habit of avoiding obvious mistakes. None of these is impressive in isolation. Repeated across years and across decisions, they separate outcomes enormously, because, again, each small edge compounds on the last.

This reframes what I'm hunting for. I'm not looking for the company that goes up the most next year. I'm looking for the business that can keep reinvesting at good returns for a long time, and that I can hold while it does. Durability of the edge matters more than the size of the edge.

## The enemies of compounding

If compounding is the engine, most of my job is removing the things that stall it. The enemies are quieter than people expect:

- **Interruption.** Selling a great compounder early, for a small profit, out of boredom, or to chase something newer, resets the clock. The most expensive trades are often the ones that felt smart at the time.
- **Impatience.** Demanding that the process pay off on your schedule rather than its own.
- **Fees and friction.** Costs and taxes compound too, just against you. A small leak repeated for decades drains a lot.
- **Fear.** Panic-selling at the bottom converts a temporary paper decline into a permanent loss, and removes you from the recovery.
- **Churn.** Constant trading feels like activity but usually just transfers the base to the broker and the tax office.

Notice that none of these are about picking better. They're about *not interrupting* something that was already working. Most of the damage I've seen, in others and in myself, comes from breaking the chain, not from choosing wrong at the start.

## How it shapes my decisions

Once I take compounding seriously, a few things follow naturally.

I weight **how long** an advantage can last as heavily as how big it is today. I'm suspicious of anything that requires constant trading to work. I try to do less, not more, because every action is a chance to interrupt the process. And I try to extend my own horizon deliberately, because the longer I'm willing to wait, the more the math works in my favour.

I also try to compound the non-financial things on purpose, reading widely, writing my reasoning down, reflecting on mistakes, because those edges feed back into the investing edge. The accounts and the mind compound on the same principle.

## The quiet version

Compounding rarely looks dramatic while it's happening. It looks like patience, consistency, and a slightly boring refusal to interfere. The drama is all backloaded, and it only arrives for people who stayed.

So when I think about compounding, I'm really thinking about a single discipline: find something with a durable edge, avoid the things that break the chain, and then have the patience to leave it alone long enough for time to do the rest.

*Nothing here is advice. These are personal notes about how I think.*
