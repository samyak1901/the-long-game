# Alphabet: Distribution, AI, and the Search Moat

There is a simple question underneath the entire Alphabet debate: is Google being disrupted by AI, or is the market badly underestimating how much distribution, data, and default behaviour actually matter? I think it's mostly the second, and this essay is my attempt to say why, in plain language, before I get lost in the numbers.

*This is the narrative version of my thinking. The figures live in the [Alphabet pitch note](/pitches/alphabet); here I want the argument.*

## What Alphabet actually is

It's tempting to describe Alphabet as a sprawling technology conglomerate. It's more useful to describe it as one enormous advertising business with a software empire wrapped around it.

The core engine is search advertising, and it works because it monetises *intent*. When someone types "best car insurance" or "plumber near me," they are telling Google exactly what they want to buy. Matching that intent to an advertiser is among the highest-return advertising that has ever existed, which is why advertisers keep paying and why the economics are so hard to replicate.

Around that engine sits everything else: YouTube, an audience and ad business approaching the scale of the largest streamers; Google Cloud, growing quickly and increasingly profitable; and a portfolio of products, Maps, Gmail, Drive, Android, Chrome, that billions of people touch every day.

## The moat is distribution, not just the algorithm

People talk about Google's moat as if it were purely technical, the best search algorithm. The algorithm matters, but the deeper moat is **distribution and default behaviour**.

Chrome is the world's browser. Android is the world's mobile OS. Google is the default search on enormous swathes of the internet. The result is reach to billions of people at effectively zero marginal acquisition cost. A competitor with a comparable model still has to *get in front of users*, and that means paying third parties, building hardware, or buying their way to attention, all of which costs real money and time.

This is the part the disruption narrative tends to skip. Even a genuinely excellent rival product doesn't automatically get distribution. Defaults are sticky, habits are stickier, and the data advantage feeds back on itself:

> Best data → better results → more usage → more data. The loop is the moat.

## The AI fear, examined

The bear case is loud and roughly this: conversational AI replaces the search box, users stop clicking ten blue links, and Google's golden funnel collapses.

I take the risk seriously, but I think it's overstated, for a few reasons.

- Google has been building world-class models for years; the capability gap that markets feared in the early ChatGPT panic was never as wide as the headlines suggested.
- AI answers and high-intent commercial queries are not the same product. Knowing the capital of a country is a query AI handles beautifully and that Google barely monetised anyway. "Which mortgage should I get" is a query with an advertiser attached.
- Whoever wins consumer AI still needs *distribution*, and Google already owns the surfaces (browser, phone, apps) where that AI would live.

So the more likely path, to me, is that AI reshapes the interface of search rather than deletes the business underneath it. The monetisation may move around; the underlying advantage, intent plus distribution, travels with it.

## Shots on goal

What makes Alphabet interesting rather than merely cheap is the optionality stacked on top of the core.

- **YouTube** is still arguably under-monetised relative to how much attention it commands.
- **Cloud** is reaching the scale where it stops being a cash drain and starts being a profit contributor.
- **Waymo** is early, but autonomous mobility is the kind of long-dated bet that, if it works, is very large.
- Lightly monetised products (Maps, Gmail) are levers the company simply hasn't fully pulled.

None of these need to be heroic for the thesis to work. They're free-ish call options attached to a business that already generates enormous cash.

## What would change my mind

Honesty means writing down the disconfirming signals in advance:

- Clear evidence that AI answers are *structurally* eroding high-intent commercial search, not just informational queries.
- Capital spending that keeps climbing for years without a visible return, suggesting the AI build-out is value-destructive rather than defensive.
- A genuine, durable loss of distribution, a world where Google is no longer the default people reach for.
- Regulation that forcibly breaks the distribution loop (the antitrust risk is real and recurring).

If those show up, the thesis weakens, and I'd want to know it early.

## Why I'm comfortable

Strip it back and the bet is straightforward. Alphabet is a dominant, cash-generative business trading at an undemanding multiple for its quality, with a distribution moat that the dominant market narrative is actively discounting, and a handful of large optional upsides on top. The fear is loud; the moat is quiet. I'm happy to own the quiet thing.
