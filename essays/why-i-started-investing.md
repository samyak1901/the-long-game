# Why I Started Investing

I did not start investing because I wanted to trade charts or chase quick wins. I started because of a single idea that I still find quietly astonishing: that an ordinary person, with no special access and no inside information, can own a small piece of an extraordinary business and let it work for years.

That idea reframed everything. A share stopped being a ticker that goes up and down on a screen and became what it actually is, a fractional claim on a real company, its factories and brands and people, its profits this year and, more importantly, its profits a decade from now. Once I saw it that way, I couldn't unsee it.

## From speculation to ownership

Like most people, my first instinct was the wrong one. I thought investing was about prediction, guessing which way the price would move next week and being right more often than wrong. That framing is exhausting and, for me, unwinnable. It turns the market into a casino where the house edge is my own impatience.

The shift that mattered was moving from *speculating on prices* to *owning businesses*. A speculator asks, "Will this go up soon?" An owner asks, "Is this a good business, run by capable people, that I'd be happy to hold even if the market closed for five years?" Those are completely different questions, and only one of them has answers I can actually research.

When I think like an owner, volatility stops being a threat and starts being an opportunity. A great business getting cheaper is not a problem to flee; it's the thing I claimed I wanted, finally on sale.

## Why businesses fascinate me

The deeper I went, the more I realised the investing was almost secondary. What hooked me was the businesses themselves.

- Why do some companies earn high returns for decades while competitors with more money and talent can't break in?
- How does a moat actually form, through brand, network effects, switching costs, scale, or just relentless execution?
- How do incentives shape behaviour, all the way from the CEO's pay package down to the salesperson's quota?

Studying a business is studying the real world with the lights on. You learn how value is created, where it leaks, and why patience and good incentives compound into something durable. Investing just happens to be the discipline that forces you to answer those questions with money on the line, which is the only way I've found to keep myself honest.

## Why I write it down

This is the part most people skip, and it's the part I think matters most.

It is easy to *feel* like I understand a company. It is much harder to write down, in plain language, why I own it: what the business does, why it should be worth more over time, what I'm paying, and, crucially, what would prove me wrong. The act of writing exposes the gaps. Hand-wavy reasoning survives in my head; it does not survive a paragraph.

> If I can't explain the thesis simply, I don't really have one yet.

Writing also creates a record. A year later I can read my own words and ask whether the thesis still holds or whether I'm quietly moving the goalposts to justify a position I've grown attached to. My memory will happily rewrite history to make me look smart. The written page won't. It keeps me accountable to my past self, which is the version of me with the clearest, least emotional view.

That is really what this site is for. **The Long Game** is my thinking, made public so I can't cheat at it.

## Thinking in years, not weeks

The hardest habit to build, and the most valuable, was lengthening my time horizon. Most market noise, the daily moves, the breathless headlines, the quarter that missed by a penny, simply doesn't matter to an owner measuring in years. Stretching the horizon filters almost all of it out.

It also changes what I look for. Over weeks, price is mostly mood. Over years, price tends to follow the business: its earnings, its reinvestment, its ability to compound. If I get the business roughly right and pay a sensible price, time does most of the heavy lifting. My job is mainly to understand, to be patient, and to avoid unforced errors.

That last part is underrated. A lot of long-term investing is simply *not* doing the dumb thing, not panic-selling, not chasing, not over-trading, not betting the farm on a single idea. The returns come from sitting still on good decisions far longer than feels natural.

## What comes next

So this is the starting point. I care about investing because I care about businesses, and I care about businesses because they're the clearest example I know of patience and incentives compounding over time. Writing keeps me honest; a long horizon keeps me calm.

Everything else on this site, the essays, the company notes, even the football, comes back to those few ideas. I'm not trying to be right this quarter. I'm trying to understand a handful of things deeply, hold them through the noise, and let time do what it does.

*Nothing here is advice. These are personal notes about how I think.*
